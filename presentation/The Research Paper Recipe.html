02

Before you write

Build the argument before you build the prose

The schema is the chain from hypothesis to evidence to interpretation. Build it first and you find the holes before they are embedded in polished sentences.

Intent layer

What is the central claim, who is the reader, and what should they understand after reading?

Evidence layer

What data supports each part of the claim, what is the comparison, and what would falsify it?

Structural layer

Which sections exist, what does each one do, and in what order must each be confirmed?

Miss a layer and the hole survives — fluently — into the prose.

11

Measurement

Five questions to ask of any outcome measure

Apply them both ways — to your own study, and to every study you plan to cite.

1

Aligned to the intervention?

Aligned tests reliably inflate effect sizes.

2

What is the baseline comparison?

Every effect size is a difference — against what?

3

What population does the sample represent?

Your sample is your sample, not the population you invoke.

4

What timescale — retrieval or storage?

Immediate performance is not durable retention.

5

If the hypothesis were false, would this design detect it?

A study that confirms regardless is theater, not a test.

The Research Paper Recipe
16

Data quality & statistics

Why large samples break the p-value

Two denominators, often confused

SD

Spread of the data — how different people are. Does not shrink as n grows.

SE = SD / √n

Precision of the mean estimate. Shrinks as n grows.

t = Δ / SE

n ↑→SE ↓→t ↑→p ↓

10,000 employees · app cuts stress 0.4 on a 100-point scale

p = .0001
looks decisive
d = 0.04
practically zero

Fisher built the p-value in the 1920s for 30-row field plots, where clearing t ≈ 2 was a real filter. With millions of rows, p < .05 is a formality — not a filter.

17

Data quality & statistics

Degrees of freedom — and t versus Z

df = n − constraints. It sets the shape of the t-distribution — and the framework is self-calibrating.

Fewer df → fatter tails → a larger t is needed to clear significance.

Z

Assumes you know the population SD (σ). Rare.

t

Estimates SD from your sample. Almost always.

df = 60t 2.00 · Z 1.96
df ≈ 200agree to 3 digits
df = ∞t = Z

Gosset published the t-distribution as “Student” at the Guinness brewery in 1908 — built for tiny samples. As df grows, t converges to Z on its own; you never switch by hand.

Data quality & statistics

Effect size is the honest number

d = (M₁ − M₂) / SD

The denominator is SD, not SE — so √n never enters. Sample size cannot inflate it.

n = 30 n = 30,000 ≈ the same d

…but wildly different t and p.

Cohen’s benchmarks for d
0.2
small
0.5
medium
0.8
large

A starting point — magnitude is always judged in context.

The ASA formally warned against over-reliance on p < .05 in 2016. Effect size measures the phenomenon itself — report it, always.

Data quality & statistics

“Could this be noise?” is not “does this matter?”

Two different questions

p-value

“Could this be noise?” — is the effect real?

Effect size

“Does this matter?” — is the difference important?

50,000 employees · wellness app cuts stress 0.3 on a 100-point scale

p < .0001
the effect is “real”
d = 0.03
nobody’s life changed

At large n, p almost always says “yes, real.” That answer stops being a filter — the only interesting question left is magnitude.

At large n, statistical significance is nearly guaranteed. Practical significance requires human judgment — not a threshold.

Results — OK

The Research Paper Recipe
28

Drafting · Discussion

The Discussion is the argument. Argue.

1

Central finding + meaning. State the central finding in one sentence — not a results restatement. Then answer: so what? What does this finding change, resolve, or challenge? This is the only sentence a reviewer should need to understand your contribution.

2

Connect to prior work. Cite specifically — not decoratively. For each cited study: does your finding agree, extend, contradict, or refine it? A citation without a stance is not an argument. “Consistent with Smith et al.” is not analysis.

3

Address null & unexpected results. Every null result and every surprise gets a sentence. Explain it — don’t bury it. A discussion that only addresses significant findings is not a discussion, it is a press release.

4

Limitations + implications. Name the two or three real limits — sample, design, timescale. Then give specific implications: for whom, doing what, under which conditions. “More research is needed” is not an implication.

Opening with a results restatement

Ending with “more research is needed”

Drafting · Introduction

Three moves: territory → niche → occupation

Move 1 · TerritoryThe domain and why it matters
Move 2 · NicheWhat the field still does not know — the unresolved gap in the literature
Move 3 · OccupationHow this paper answers exactly that gap
Move 2 · Niche
Too broad

“Despite growing interest in AI tutoring, little research has examined its effects on student learning.”

Specific

“No study has tested whether AI feedback specificity — not frequency — drives the retention gap between immediate and delayed post-tests in undergraduate programming courses.”

Move 3 · Occupation
Too broad

“This study investigates AI tutoring in undergraduate programming courses.”

Specific

“This study isolates feedback specificity as the independent variable in a randomized trial with 91 undergraduates, measuring both immediate and six-week delayed retention.”

If the niche could motivate any study in the area, rewrite it.

If the occupation doesn’t answer the niche directly, rewrite both.

Ends here — a clear, testable research question or hypothesis.

Match your language to your evidence

“raises the possibility”single case study
“suggests”one correlational study
“indicates”correlational, large N
“demonstrates”randomized experiment
“proves”RCT, multi-site, replicated
Lower confidence · exploratoryWell-established

Your language is the claim. Choose it as carefully as the hypothesis — then audit every “causes,” “leads to,” and “produces.”

Fog is the distance between what a sentence says and what it means

Nominalization“The investigation of the relationship was conducted.”
“We investigated how X relates to Y.”
Vague quantifier“Many students improved.”
“74 of 91 students (81%) improved.”
Throat-clearing“It is important to note that the effect was large.”

Over-hedging“Results were somewhat significant.”
“p = .02, d = 0.41.”

Fog test — is every word earning its place?
