## Chapter 9 — Literature Review

### Verified

- **Swales' CARS model — attribution and the three moves.** Correctly attributed in the chapter. John Swales developed the "Create a Research Space" (CARS) model in *Genre Analysis: English in Academic and Research Settings* (Cambridge University Press, 1990). The three rhetorical moves are exactly as the chapter states: **Move 1** establish a territory (importance + prior research), **Move 2** establish a niche (gap, counter-claim, question-raising, or continuing a tradition), **Move 3** occupy the niche (announce purpose/method/findings). The chapter's claim that weak intros fail at Move 2 is consistent with the model and with the standard pedagogy around it. The four niche-establishing sub-types the chapter lists (counter-claiming, gap-indicating, question-raising, continuing a tradition) match Swales' own typology. **Verified.**
- **Citation-hallucination warning (the chapter's "hard rule").** The chapter's claim that AI models fabricate plausible-but-nonexistent citations is well supported, not anecdotal. Multiple peer-reviewed studies quantify it (see *New to add*). The chapter's framing — "a provenance failure," not a formatting error — is sound and now empirically backable. **Verified and strengthenable.**
- **Synthesis-vs-summary distinction and the synthesis matrix.** These are standard, well-established literature-review pedagogy (taught across university library guides and writing-center materials); no factual claim to flag. **Verified as established practice.**
- **Citation monoculture / publication bias toward positive results.** The mechanism described (convergence that is methodological rather than substantive; positive-result publication bias) is established. No correction needed.

### Corrections

- **None factually required.** One precision note: the chapter says CARS "describes what the best introductions actually do rather than prescribing a format." Accurate — Swales derived CARS descriptively from a corpus of real introductions. Keep that framing; it is a genuine strength and distinguishes CARS from a template.

### New to add (full citations, verifiable)

1. **Swales, J. M. (1990). *Genre Analysis: English in Academic and Research Settings*. Cambridge University Press.** — The primary source for CARS; cite it directly rather than secondary library guides. Swales & Feak's *Academic Writing for Graduate Students* (3rd ed., University of Michigan Press, 2012) is the standard pedagogical companion that operationalizes CARS for student writers — worth a footnote.
2. **Page, M. J., McKenzie, J. E., Bossuyt, P. M., et al. (2021). "The PRISMA 2020 statement: an updated guideline for reporting systematic reviews." *BMJ*, 372:n71.** https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n71 — The current standard for *structured/systematic* literature reviews (27-item checklist + abstract checklist). Lets the chapter point students past the informal synthesis matrix toward a formal reporting standard when the review is itself the study. Pairs naturally with the matrix tool.
3. **Gravel, J., D'Amours-Gravel, M., & Osmanlliu, E. (2023). "Learning to Fake It: Limited Responses and Fabricated References Provided by ChatGPT for Medical Questions." *Mayo Clinic Proceedings: Digital Health* / and Walters, W. H. & Wilder, E. I. (2023). "Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT." *Scientific Reports*, 13:14045.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5 — Walters & Wilder is the cleanest peer-reviewed quantification: of citations generated by GPT-3.5, ~55% were fabricated; for GPT-4, ~18% were fabricated, and even genuine references contained substantial citation errors. Use as the empirical backbone for the chapter's "hard rule." (Note rates are model- and version-dependent and falling over time, so cite the rate as illustrative, not fixed.)

### Deck beats to fold in

- The deck frames the lit review as **step 4 in the research order** (after question-refinement, before methods design) — i.e., it situates the chapter inside a lifecycle the chapter itself doesn't draw. The chapter teaches CARS beautifully but treats the review as a writing task; the deck's sharper move is showing the review is *also* a doing-order step that feeds methods design. Worth a single cross-reference sentence.
- The deck explicitly ties **CARS Move 2 (niche) to the refined research question** — "the literature review is where the refined question gets situated." The chapter gestures at this ("the hypothesis should feel inevitable") but the deck's link to question-refinement (FINER) is a tighter through-line. The research-order-notes.md file already plans a FINER/PICO treatment upstream; ch09 could add one sentence handing back to it.

## Chapter 10 — Drafting (Section by Section)

### Verified

- **Writing-in-discovery-order advice (Methods → Results → Discussion → Introduction → Abstract → Title).** Authoritatively backed. George M. Whitesides' widely circulated "Whitesides' Group: Writing a Paper" (*Advanced Materials*, 16(15):1375–1377, 2004) argues you should outline and draft around the data first, treating text as secondary to figures/tables, and build the introduction's framing once the results are known. The chapter's central claim — draft in the order that protects honesty, not the order readers expect — is consistent with established science-writing pedagogy. **Verified.**
- **Reporting standards (JARS, CONSORT, STROBE).** All correctly attributed. JARS = APA Journal Article Reporting Standards (Appelbaum et al., 2018, *American Psychologist*, for quantitative research); CONSORT for randomized trials; STROBE for observational epidemiology. The chapter's logic ("readers cannot evaluate findings without design details") is exactly these standards' rationale. **Verified.**
- **Results = report what happened, no interpretation ("suggests"/"demonstrates" belong in Discussion).** Standard and correct; consistent with all major reporting guidance. **Verified.**
- **Discussion structure — open with the central finding, do not restate results, do not close with "more research is needed."** Verified against multiple writing-guidance sources (USC Libraries writing guide; University of Guelph "Writing in the Sciences"; AJE). The first Discussion paragraph should state the main finding and connect it to the gap; bare results-restatement openings and "more research is needed" closings are explicitly flagged as weak moves. The chapter's "4 moves" framing is sound practitioner pedagogy. **Verified as established practice** (note: the specific "4 moves" enumeration is a useful editorial synthesis, not a single named canonical model like CARS — fine to present as the book's own framework).
- **Abstract = stand-alone compression; most common failure is misalignment with Results.** The "abstract must stand alone and match the paper" requirement is universal guidance. The deck's sharper claim — that **the finding is the element most often missing** — is supported by the abstract-quality literature (see *New to add*): structured abstracts routinely omit key results, effect sizes, and confidence intervals.

### Corrections — flag the one overstatement

- **The "experts read figures/tables and Discussion first" claim (slides 25–32; chapter §28).** The chapter states this as fact: "the eye goes to the figures and tables... then to the Discussion... and only if the result is worth scrutinizing do they return to the Methods and, last of all, the Introduction." **This is well-attested practitioner wisdom, NOT an empirical reading-behavior study.** The best citable source is Elisabeth Pain's "How to (seriously) read a scientific paper," *Science* (AAAS, 2016), which compiles working scientists' self-reported strategies — many report reading abstract → figures → discussion, and several say the figures are the most trustworthy part because text can be "shaped to tell a compelling story." That is survey-of-practitioners evidence, not eye-tracking or controlled reading-behavior research. **Recommendation:** keep the claim — it is sound and useful — but soften the empirical register. Frame it as "experienced readers report" / "the common practice among working scientists is," and cite the *Science* piece. Do **not** assert it as a measured fact about all expert readers. The chapter's existing hedge ("Few researchers read front to back") is good; the specific ordered sequence is the part stated too confidently.

### New to add (full citations, verifiable)

1. **Whitesides, G. M. (2004). "Whitesides' Group: Writing a Paper." *Advanced Materials*, 16(15), 1375–1377.** https://doi.org/10.1002/adma.200400767 — The authoritative backing for the discovery-order / outline-from-data advice and the "text is secondary to figures" point. A natural citation for the chapter's core thesis and for its Chapter-8 figures cross-reference.
2. **Pain, E. (2016). "How to (seriously) read a scientific paper." *Science* (AAAS).** https://www.science.org/content/article/how-seriously-read-scientific-paper — The practitioner-wisdom source for the figures-first / discussion-second reading behavior. Label as compiled self-report from working scientists, not empirical reading-behavior measurement.
3. **Appelbaum, M., Cooper, H., Kline, R. B., Mayo-Wilson, E., Nezu, A. M., & Rao, S. M. (2018). "Journal article reporting standards for quantitative research in psychology: The APA Publications and Communications Board Task Force Report." *American Psychologist*, 73(1), 3–25.** https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0000191 — The current JARS citation (the chapter names JARS but gives no source). For the abstract-quality point, supplement with **Hartley, J. (2014). "Current findings from research on structured abstracts: an update." *Journal of the Medical Library Association*, 102(3), 146–148** (PMC4076121) — documents persistent omission of results/findings in abstracts even under structured formats, backing the deck's "finding most often missing" claim.

### Deck beats to fold in

- **The deck makes the two reading orders explicit and names them (naive vs. expert); the chapter folds both into one prose paragraph.** The chapter's §28 actually *is* sharper than most treatments (it already distinguishes naive linear reading from expert figures-first reading) — credit where due. But the deck's clean three-way split (naive reading order / expert reading order / writing order, all as distinct ordered lists) is more legible than the chapter's running prose. The planned infographic comment in the chapter (`drafting order vs reading order`) should show all THREE orders, not two, to match the deck.
- **The deck's Discussion "4 moves" are crisper than the chapter's prose.** The chapter covers all four moves (central finding + meaning; connect to prior work with a stance; address null/unexpected; limitations + implications) but spreads them across several paragraphs. The deck states them as an explicit ordered checklist with two named anti-patterns (never open with results restatement; never close with "more research is needed"). The chapter mentions the "more research is needed" anti-pattern in **Chapter 9** (re: niche) but not in the Chapter-10 Discussion section — folding the deck's explicit 4-move list into ch10 would close that gap.
- **The deck's abstract = "5 sentences (problem, gap, method, finding, implication)" with "finding most often missing"** is more concrete than the chapter's "miniature version of the argument." The 5-sentence skeleton is a usable student tool; the chapter's abstract section would be sharper with it. (The "finding most often missing" diagnosis is now citable via Hartley 2014.)
