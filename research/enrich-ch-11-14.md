## Chapter 11 — Quality Control

### Verified
- **GRIM test attribution.** Ch11 line 48 cites "GRIM (Granularity-Related Inconsistency of Means)" detecting impossible means given sample size and scale. Correct. Brown & Heathers (2017), *Social Psychological and Personality Science* 8(4): 363–369. The chapter's claim that GRIM-class methods have "identified systematic reporting errors in published research" is accurate: of 71 testable articles in their sample, ~half (36) had ≥1 inconsistent mean; data requests confirmed reporting errors in all 9 datasets received. The chapter's wording is appropriately calibrated — it does not overclaim fraud, it says "some are mistakes... some are errors introduced during transcription."
- **The "AI polish makes unsupported claims harder to detect" argument** (lines 60–70) is a reasonable inference consistent with the processing-fluency literature (fluent text is judged more true/credible). The chapter frames it as "a description of how human cognition works," which is the right epistemic register — it does not cite a specific study and does not need to, but a fluency citation is available if wanted (see New to add).
- **Sequencing claim** (audit logic before AI prose editing) is the chapter's own pedagogy and is internally consistent with the rest of the book; no external standard contradicts it.

### Corrections
- None required. No factual errors found. Minor: "Granularity-Related Inconsistency of Means" is the standard expansion; some sources render it "Granularity-Related Inconsistency of Means" identically — fine as written.

## Chapter 12 — Peer-Review Simulation

### Verified
- **"Reviewer agreement is often not much better than chance"** (line 22, "a landmark analysis documented..."). Verified and a real, citable result. **Rothwell, P. M., & Martyn, C. N. (2000),** "Reproducibility of peer review in clinical neuroscience: Is agreement between reviewers any greater than would be expected by chance alone?" *Brain*, 123(9), 1964–1969 — found inter-reviewer agreement at or near chance. Corroborated by a meta-analysis: **Bornmann, Mutz & Daniel (2010),** PLOS ONE 5(12): e14331, reporting low pooled inter-rater reliability. The chapter currently leaves "a landmark analysis" unnamed; it can now be named.
- **ICMJE has addressed AI in research writing** (line 84): correct and current. ICMJE *Recommendations* updated January 2025 (building on the May 2023 / January 2024 versions); authors must disclose AI use, AI cannot be an author. Accurate as written.
- **The constraint that some journals prohibit uploading manuscripts to third-party AI for review** (lines 78–82): **VERIFIED and current.** This is now a hard, named policy, not a soft caution:
  - **NIH** banned use of generative AI tools in peer review of grant applications, effective June 23, 2023 — uploading application content to online generative AI violates confidentiality requirements.
  - **ICMJE** explicitly recommends reviewers "not upload manuscripts to software or other AI technology platforms that cannot guarantee confidentiality."
  - **Science (AAAS)** prohibits reviewers from uploading manuscripts to generative AI tools.
  The chapter's hedge ("Some journals explicitly prohibit...") is now an understatement — it can be stated more firmly with citations.

### Corrections
- **Soften-to-firm, not an error:** Line 80's "Some journals explicitly prohibit uploading unpublished manuscripts to third-party AI systems" undersells the current state. By 2024–2026 this is a near-consensus position among major funders and publishers (NIH, ICMJE, Science, CMAJ). Recommend strengthening to reflect that this is now standard policy across major venues, with named examples — still keeping "check your specific venue."

- **NIH (2023).** "The Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence Technologies is Prohibited for the NIH Peer Review Process." ## Chapter 13 — Ethics and Bias Screen

### Verified
- **COPE and ICMJE both hold AI cannot be an author; humans remain accountable** (lines 20–22). **VERIFIED and current.**
  - **COPE Position Statement on Authorship and AI Tools (Feb 13, 2023):** AI tools cannot be listed as authors; they cannot take responsibility for the work, assert conflicts of interest, or manage copyright/licensing. Use must be disclosed (in Methods/Materials or equivalent). https://publicationethics.org/guidance/cope-position/authorship-and-ai-tools
  - **ICMJE (Jan 2025 update):** AI cannot be an author; significant AI use must be disclosed (e.g., acknowledgments); authors remain fully responsible for accuracy and integrity.
  The chapter's framing — "AI tools cannot be listed as authors, because authorship carries accountability" — is exactly right.
- **"Different journals have different thresholds; some still developing policies; check current author guidelines"** (line 22): accurate and well-hedged. This is the correct posture given how fast the policies move.
- **Fanelli citation** (line 56): "Fanelli demonstrated that the proportion of positive results in published papers has increased over time." Verified. **Fanelli, D. (2010),** "'Positive' Results Increase Down the Hierarchy of the Sciences," *PLOS ONE* 5(4): e10068, established the cross-disciplinary positive-results gradient. The *increase over time* specifically is **Fanelli (2012),** "Negative results are disappearing from most disciplines and countries," *Scientometrics* 90: 891–904 — that is the paper that documents the temporal trend the chapter describes. **Minor attribution note:** the chapter's "increased over time" claim maps most precisely to Fanelli 2012, not the 2010 hierarchy paper. Cite 2012 (or both) for the temporal claim.

### Corrections
- **Attribution precision (Fanelli):** The sentence "Fanelli demonstrated that the proportion of positive results in published papers has increased over time" is best supported by **Fanelli (2012)**, *Scientometrics* — the "negative results are disappearing" paper — rather than the 2010 PLOS ONE hierarchy paper, which is about cross-discipline variation, not the time trend. If the chapter intends the time-trend claim, cite 2012. Not a factual error, a citation-precision fix.

## Chapter 14 — Writing Well: Prose for Scientific Papers

### Verified
- **Gopen & Swan, reader-expectation / topic-stress positions** (lines 74–80). **VERIFIED, citation is real and correctly characterized.** Gopen, G. D., & Swan, J. A. (1990), "The Science of Scientific Writing," *American Scientist*, 78(6): 550–558. The chapter's summary — readers expect familiar context in the topic (subject) position and new important information in the stress (end) position — is an accurate rendering of the paper's central principle ("if the reader is to grasp what the writer means, the writer must understand what the reader needs"). Page range 550–558 confirmed.
- **Williams & Bizup, actor-action / make-the-character-the-subject principle** (lines 82). **VERIFIED.** Williams, J. M., & Bizup, J., *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* (now in its 12th-13th editions; Bizup co-authors recent editions). The chapter's rendering — find the central actor and central action, make the actor the grammatical subject and the action the main verb — is exactly Williams's two core principles. Correct.
- **Nominalization concept** (lines 18–28). Accurate. The chapter's -tion/-ment/-ance/-ence/-ity/-al test and "not all nominalizations are problems" caveat are sound and match the standard treatment. The chapter does NOT currently credit Helen Sword's "zombie nouns," which is the most quotable popular framing of this exact idea — an enrichment opportunity, not a correction.
- **Epistemic-verb scale** (demonstrates > shows > indicates > suggests > is consistent with, lines 56–66). This ordering is consistent with the hedging/boosting literature (Hyland): "demonstrate" is a booster, "suggest"/"may" are hedges. The *ordinal* scale is reasonable and defensible. **But the precise verb→design mapping is the book's own heuristic, not a codified standard** (see below).
- **"Over-hedging is as dishonest as overclaiming"** (the deck's phrasing; Ch14 lines 68 "defensive hedging... hiding, not disclosing"). This is well-supported by Hyland's account that hedges carry genuine epistemic content — emptying a claim of all commitment is not neutrality, it's evasion. The chapter's "calibrated hedging names the actual uncertainty; defensive hedging names no uncertainty at all" is a strong, defensible formulation.

### Corrections
- **No factual errors.** One labeling recommendation: the chapter presents the epistemic-verb scale as if it were settled practice. It should be flagged (here, and ideally in-text) that **the specific mapping of each verb to a specific study design is a useful pedagogical heuristic, not a published standard.** No authority (APA, Hyland, Gopen-Swan, Williams) publishes a verb→design lookup table. APA 7th edition cautions against causal language outpacing design and emphasizes precision, but does not codify "demonstrates = RCT." The literature supports the *direction* (match verb strength to evidence strength) but not the *exact rungs*. The book is free to use the ladder — it's good pedagogy — but should present it as its own heuristic.

### New to add
- **Gopen, G. D., & Swan, J. A. (1990).** "The Science of Scientific Writing." *American Scientist*, 78(6), 550–558. — formalize the citation the chapter already paraphrases.
- **Williams, J. M., & Bizup, J. (2017).** *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* (12th ed.). Pearson. — formalize the "Williams and Bizup principle" the chapter names.
- **Sword, H. (2012).** "Zombie Nouns." *The New York Times* (Opinionator), July 23, 2012; and *Stylish Academic Writing* (Harvard University Press, 2012). — the most memorable framing of the nominalization problem ("zombie nouns... cannibalize active verbs"). High-value addition for the nominalization section; gives students a vivid handle and a TED-Ed video.
- **Hyland, K. (1998).** *Hedging in Scientific Research Articles.* John Benjamins; and Hyland, K. (2005), *Metadiscourse: Exploring Interaction in Writing*, Continuum. — authoritative backing for the hedges-vs-boosters framing and for "over-hedging is dishonest." This is the scholarly anchor for the chapter's epistemic-verb section.

### Deck beats to fold in
- The deck's **fog audit** is a tighter, named checklist than Ch14's prose treatment: (1) nominalization, (2) vague quantifier, (3) throat-clearing / "it is important to note," (4) over-hedging. **Ch14 covers nominalization, vague quantifiers, and hedging well but is MISSING the deck's "throat-clearing / it is important to note" item entirely.** That is a clean gap — the deck is sharper here. Worth adding a short throat-clearing section (empty openers: "It is important to note that," "It should be mentioned that," "Interestingly,") as a fourth fog source.

Ch14's "prose quality is the last thing to address" is correct but the deck makes the gate structure explicit. Ch14 line 86–88 already gestures at this ("comes after the claim audit, the peer-review simulation, and the ethics screen") — folding in the explicit phase-gate language would strengthen it.
